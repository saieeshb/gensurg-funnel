The IMG funnel is a different market, not a harder version of the same one

Non-US IMGs made up 7.2% of reported invitations in 2025–26, and more than half of those were for preliminary positions. The ones who did get invited scored ten points higher than the US MDs who got invited. Every stage of the funnel behaves differently.

83 Non-US IMG invites, 2025–26 84 rows in the dedicated IMG tab 30 IMG applicants with outcomes, 2024–25

Read this first — the sample is small

The 2024–25 workbook has 30 IMG applicants, of whom 15 reported a match outcome and 15 reported signal conversion. Those are the numbers behind the outcome and signal charts on this page. They are too small to estimate a rate for anyone, and a handful of different respondents would move them noticeably.

A second limitation matters just as much: the 2024–25 workbook does not separate US IMGs from non-US IMGs. It has one “IMG” category. Only the 2025–26 invitation log distinguishes them. So the invitation-side findings below are genuinely about non-US IMGs; the outcome-side findings are about IMGs as a single mixed group, and the two are labelled accordingly throughout.

FINDING 01

Most of what looks like an interview is a preliminary interview

The single largest structural difference, and the one most likely to be misread as good news in October.

Verdict

56.6% of Non-US IMG invitations were for preliminary positions, against 1.9% for US MDs — a thirtyfold difference. Counting invitations without separating the two tracks will badly overstate where a non-US IMG actually stands.

83 Non-US IMG invitations χ² = 318, p ≈ 3×10⁻⁷¹ How track was inferred

FINDING 02

The score bar is not the same bar

The clearest evidence on this page that the filter is different, not merely tighter.

Verdict

Non-US IMGs who received an invitation had a median Step 2 CK of 265, against 255 for US MDs who received one. 57.8% of Non-US IMG invitees scored 260 or above, against 44.9% of MD invitees. Same invitation, more score required to earn it.

83 vs 816 invitations with a recorded score Mann–Whitney p = 0.00004

FINDING 03

The arithmetic of a 1.2% hit rate

This is the number that reframes every other decision, including whether the standard advice about signals applies at all.

Verdict

IMG applicants sent a median of 137 applications and received a median of 1 interview — a hit rate of 1.2%, against 15.6% for US MDs sending half as many. Programme signals converted at 2.40 of 15 against 6.51 for US MDs.

n = 30 IMG applicants yield p = 3×10⁻⁹ signals p = 0.00003

What this changes about signal strategy

On the findings page, the advice is to concentrate signals where you fit. For a non-US IMG that advice needs a correction: the programmes that invite non-US IMGs at all are a much smaller set than the programmes you are eligible to apply to. Spending signals on programmes with no recent history of taking non-US IMGs is the most common way the 15 are wasted, and the programme list above is a better starting point than a general competitiveness tier.

The dedicated IMG tab records % of its invitees as having done a research year. It shows no association with getting a signalled invitation (Fisher OR 0.85, p = 0.82) — but that tests whether research predicts which invitations were signalled, not whether research produced invitations at all. The workbooks cannot answer the second question, and it is the one that matters.

DECISIONS

What to do differently

Weaker evidence than the main findings page, and labelled as such. Where this rests on fifteen people, it says so.

Count categorical and preliminary invitations separately, from day one

More than half of non-US IMG invitations are preliminary. An invitation count that mixes them will tell you that you are doing twice as well as you are, at exactly the moment you are deciding whether to send letters or add programmes.

56.6% vs 1.9% prelim share83 invitationsstrong evidence

Target programmes with a track record of taking non-US IMGs, not programmes at your tier

The competitiveness tier that governs signal strategy for US graduates is the wrong axis here. Eligibility and history come first: a mid-tier programme that has never taken a non-US IMG is a worse signal target than a stronger programme that takes several.

see the programme list aboveweak · small counts

Treat Step 2 CK as the one lever with clear evidence behind it

Within the IMG group it is the only measured factor that moves interview count, at 1.64× per ten points — a steeper slope than for the pool as a whole. The invitation data agrees: the non-US IMGs who got through scored ten points above the MDs who got through.

IRR 1.64 (1.18–2.26), p = 0.003moderate · n = 30

Do not expect publications to carry the application

IMG applicants already bring more publications than US MDs, a median of 9 against 6, and convert applications at one-thirteenth the rate. Nothing in either workbook shows publication count producing interviews for this group.

IRR 0.93 (0.64–1.36), p = 0.71moderatepower caveat

Decide about the preliminary route deliberately, before March

Preliminary is where 40% of the reporting IMGs landed. The sheet contains applicants who converted a prelim year into categorical invitations the following cycle, and applicants warning that it depends entirely on whether a given programme actually promotes its prelims. That is a question to ask programmes directly, during interviews.

6 of 15 reporting IMGs matched prelimweak · not measured

LIMITS

Why this page is shorter than the last one

Non-US IMGs are the most under-sampled group in both workbooks. They are 7.2% of reported invitations here, against a far larger share of the real applicant pool — these spreadsheets are written in English, circulated on US-centric forums, and filled in by people who found them. Every bias listed in the methods applies more strongly to this page than to the main findings.

The honest summary: the invitation-side findings are solid. 83 invitations is enough to establish the prelim split and the score gap, both large effects with tiny p-values. The outcome-side findings are not. Fifteen people reported a match outcome, enough to say the distribution looks very different from the US MD one, and not enough to say what an individual should expect.
